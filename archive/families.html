Foster Families

Before you open
your door.

Foster care is one of the most meaningful things a family can do. It is also one of the most demanding. This page exists to help you go in with eyes open, well prepared, and deeply rooted.

Start Here

Before anything
else, pray.

We cannot overstate this. Before you research agencies, attend an orientation, or tell your kids you're thinking about fostering — pray. Seriously and at length. Fast and pray with your spouse. Take weeks with it. Seek God's face for wisdom, clarity, and genuine conviction that this is where He is leading you.

Foster care will test your marriage, your patience, your faith, and your capacity in ways you cannot fully anticipate beforehand. The families who sustain this work over years are not the ones who were most prepared logistically. They are the ones who went in anchored to something that doesn't move when everything else does.

Go forward only when you are certain this is what God is leading you into. That certainty is worth more than any training, any agency, any support network. Start there.

Before you even begin the licensing process, there are a few honest questions worth sitting with.

Consider birth order carefully.

Placing older foster children above younger biological children can expose your kids to behaviors and language they aren't ready for. This isn't a reason not to foster — it's a reason to be honest with your agency about the ages and needs of any placement relative to your own children before you say yes.

Your marriage comes first.

If both spouses cannot honestly say "our marriage is strong," do not foster. Foster care will stress the healthiest marriages. It will fracture a struggling one. This is not a caution — it is a rule.

Know your family's current capacity.

Foster children do not hold higher value than your biological children. They hold equal value. If your family is already stretched — in parenting, in attention, in energy — be honest about what you have to give before you open your door.

Build Your Team

Please take time
to read this.

The team you build before you start is the one that keeps you going — and it needs to be in place before you're licensed.

One of the most common reasons foster families quit is isolation. Not the children. Not the system. The feeling of being completely alone in an overwhelming situation with no one to call. The answer to that is a wrap team — and it needs to exist before a child ever comes through your door.

A wrap team is a group of at least six families or individuals from your church who have given you an active, informed yes to supporting your foster family. Not a vague "let us know if you need anything" — a real commitment to show up in practical ways.

Don't be afraid to ask people in your church even if they aren't close friends. Most people are waiting to be asked. They just need to know what saying yes actually looks like.

Practical ways your team will help.

Routine Childcare

The single most valuable thing your team can provide: two or three families rotating on a weekly or biweekly schedule to take the kids (all, or just foster) for an afternoon. This gives you breathing room that is genuinely lifesaving. That rhythm of rest is what makes the rest of the week sustainable.

Everyday Practical Help

Your team should be people you can call for anything without hesitation — picking kids up from school, grabbing groceries on a brutal day, giving you a ride when your car is in the shop. The roles don't need to be formally assigned. What matters is that each person has said yes and understands what that yes might mean.

Mentors for Older Kids

Foster parents cannot be the only trusted adult in a foster child's life. Having another adult from your church who knows both your family and your foster child — who can take that kid out for lunch once a month or be a phone call away — is worth more than it might seem. A simple act of investment from a trusted adult outside the home can begin to build trust in ways a foster parent sometimes can't.

Know Before You Decide

Not all foster placements
are the same.

Tennessee's foster care system places children at three levels based on their needs. Understanding the difference before you choose an agency and open your home will help you make a decision that's honest about your family's capacity.

Children at Level 1 have minimal behavioral or emotional needs. They may have experienced neglect or instability but do not require specialized therapeutic intervention. Level 1 placements are the closest to a "typical" parenting experience within the foster care context — often younger children or children whose trauma history has not yet produced significant behavioral challenges.

Level 2 children have moderate behavioral or emotional challenges stemming from their history. They require more intentional, trauma-informed parenting and may be involved in therapy or other support services. Families taking Level 2 placements should expect more complexity in daily life and a higher administrative load — more appointments, more coordination with caseworkers and service providers.

Level 3 is the highest level of need. These children have significant trauma histories and require specialized, therapeutic parenting — intensive, consistent, and demanding. Foster parents at Level 3 receive additional training, higher per diem support, and typically work through agencies that specialize in therapeutic care and provide 24/7 on-call support. This level requires the most honest self-assessment before committing.

A note: the level of a child's placement does not determine their worth, their potential, or how much love they are capable of giving and receiving. It is simply a framework to help families match their capacity to a child's needs.
